## Origin Story

Started as a crypto wallet and payments app, then pivoted hard after early struggles. The pivot that saved the company: focusing entirely on USDC stablecoin infrastructure. Between 2018–2019, Circle sold off nearly everything else — the Poloniex exchange (to Justin Sun's group, at a loss of $156M), Circle Pay, Circle Trade, Circle Research — betting everything on USDC. That bet paid off substantially.

**Jeremy Allaire (CEO & Chairman)** — serial entrepreneur, previously founded Brightcove (video platform). Deep belief that money should function like the internet — open, programmable, borderless. Has been lobbying the US Congress for stablecoin regulation since 2023.

**Why the IPO matters:** Circle is America's first publicly traded stablecoin issuer — not just a company going public, but a form of NYSE legitimacy for the stablecoin market itself.

## What Circle Actually Does

### The Problem Being Solved

Traditional international wire transfers take 3–5 business days and cost $25–50. Cross-border payments are slow, expensive, and opaque. Small businesses and developers historically couldn't easily build payment applications on top of traditional rails — money doesn't move like information moves on the internet.

### What a Stablecoin Is

A cryptocurrency pegged 1:1 to a real currency. Unlike Bitcoin, which fluctuates, 1 USDC always equals $1.00.

**USDC's value proposition:** send $1 million internationally in seconds for cents; fully transparent (backed 1:1 by cash and US Treasuries); programmable (developers build directly on top of it); regulated and compliant, not operating in a gray area.

### Circle's Business Model

Circle issues USDC. Every unit is backed by real dollars held in reserve. Circle invests those reserves in US Treasury bonds and earns the interest — with $78B in circulation invested at roughly 4-5%, that's approximately $3-4B in annual interest income. **The clean summary:** Circle functions essentially as a money market fund that also happens to be internet infrastructure.

### Circle Is B2B Infrastructure, Not a Consumer App

Enterprises, financial institutions, and developers use Circle's APIs directly. For everyday people wanting USDC, the entry point is an exchange (Coinbase, Kraken, etc.) built on top of Circle's infrastructure — Circle is the wholesale layer, exchanges are the retail layer. The clean analogy: Circle is to stablecoins what Visa/Mastercard is to consumer payments — the infrastructure, not the consumer-facing product.

**Canada note:** Circle has limited direct Canadian consumer access; Canadian crypto regulation is stricter than US regulation; a Canadian exchange is typically the practical entry point for USDC.

## Hero Products

**USDC** — the world's second-largest stablecoin, $78B in circulation, used across crypto exchanges, DeFi applications, enterprise payments, cross-border transfers, and developer payment integrations.

**EURC** — Euro-pegged stablecoin, same underlying model applied to European markets.

**Circle Payments Network (CPN)** — one integration for global stablecoin-powered payments; businesses connect once and can send/receive USDC globally, designed to modernize cross-border payments and treasury operations.

**Arc Blockchain** — an open Layer-1 blockchain purpose-built to bring real-world economic activity onchain, explicitly positioned as an "Economic OS for the internet." This is Circle's bid to own the infrastructure layer itself, not just the stablecoin running on top of someone else's chain.

- **What Layer-1 means:** the foundational layer everything else builds on top of — Bitcoin and Ethereum are both Layer-1 blockchains. Arc is Circle's own purpose-built version, specifically for regulated, real-world money movement rather than general-purpose computing.
- **Difference from Ethereum:** Ethereum is general-purpose; Arc is purpose-built for one specific job — moving regulated money efficiently.
- **The ambition, in Circle's own words:** "We didn't just want to issue a stablecoin. We want to own the road the stablecoin travels on."

**Circle Developer Services** — APIs and tools letting developers mint, redeem, and transfer USDC programmatically inside their own applications.

## The USDC Origin Story

- **2018** — Circle and Coinbase co-founded the Centre Consortium together and launched USDC jointly as equal partners
- **August 2023** — Circle and Coinbase dissolved the Centre Consortium; Circle became the sole issuer and governor of USDC, while Coinbase retained deep distribution rights (still manages ~22% of USDC supply)
- **Context:** Circle didn't invent the stablecoin concept — Tether (USDT) launched in 2014, years earlier. Circle's competitive position is built on trust and regulatory compliance, not being first to market.

## Why IT at Circle Is High-Stakes

Circle's IT function supports engineers building APIs banks plug into, business development teams closing deals with financial institutions, legal/compliance teams navigating global regulation, and finance teams managing $78B in reserves. Downtime for these users has real financial and legal consequences — a BD team locked out of Salesforce during a $50M institutional deal, an engineer unable to push code during a critical deployment, or compliance losing access to audit tools before an SEC filing all carry real business impact well beyond typical IT inconvenience.

## Blockchain Context for IT (Not Engineering Depth Required)

An IT function supporting Circle doesn't need blockchain engineering depth — the users are blockchain engineers, but their day-to-day tooling is standard enterprise IT: GitHub, AWS, Slack, MacBooks, SSO, VPN/Zero Trust, MDM, and ServiceNow tickets like any other tech company. What matters is enough business context to support the people building the infrastructure, not building it directly.

**Plain-English blockchain basics relevant to this context:**

- USDC = a token on that database that always equals $1
- Smart contracts = code that runs automatically when conditions are met

## Technical Reference — Compliance & ITSM Architecture

### SOC 2 Trust Service Criteria (TSC)

| Criterion | What It Covers |
|---|---|
| **Security** | Protection against unauthorized access |
| **Availability** | System available as committed |
| **Processing Integrity** | Processing is complete and accurate |
| **Confidentiality** | Confidential information is protected |
| **Privacy** | Personal information handled appropriately |

**The full Security control chain, conceptually:** a GRC platform (e.g., AuditBoard) identifies a vulnerability → IT builds a remediation process → an MDM tool (e.g., Kandji) patches affected devices → evidence is logged back into the GRC platform → the Security TSC requirement is satisfied.

### AuditBoard vs. ServiceNow

AuditBoard is a dedicated GRC (Governance, Risk, Compliance) platform — distinct from ServiceNow. ServiceNow functions as IT's operating system (tickets, assets, workflows); AuditBoard functions as the compliance team's operating system (risks, controls, evidence). Device compliance data (from an MDM tool) flows into AuditBoard, mapping to specific SOC 2 controls, which an auditor then reviews as evidence.

### CMDB — Configuration Management Database

ServiceNow's master inventory of every IT asset (laptops, servers, software, network devices) and the relationships between them — the living "source of truth" for everything IT owns and manages. Without an accurate CMDB, automation is effectively guessing.

**The automation chain:** CMDB (source of truth) → ServiceNow vulnerability management (what needs fixing) → MDM patching workflows (fix it automatically) → GRC platform (evidence generated) → audit-ready.

**A healthy CMDB should be able to answer**, for example: which device in a given location is missing a critical update right now; which devices haven't checked in with the MDM within a set window; which software licenses remain active on offboarded employee devices.

### ServiceNow as System of Record — The Hub Concept

ServiceNow functions architecturally as a central hub rather than just a ticketing system — every other tool either feeds into it or receives instructions from it (HRIS → ServiceNow → identity provider → device management → collaboration tools → compliance platform, in various directions depending on the specific workflow).

### IntegrationHub / Webhooks

IntegrationHub is how ServiceNow communicates with external tools — sending and receiving data via APIs and webhooks. A webhook is a real-time notification: an event fires, the receiving system is immediately notified and can execute a downstream action (e.g., a new-hire ticket triggers automatic account creation, device enrollment, and license assignment across multiple systems).

### Service Catalog & Workflows

The Service Catalog is the "menu" employees use to request IT services (software, hardware, access, onboarding). A workflow is what happens behind the scenes after a request is submitted.

| Workflow Component | What It Does |
|---|---|
| **Trigger** | What starts the workflow — form submission, approval, date |
| **Approval step** | Who must approve before it proceeds |
| **Condition** | If/then logic (e.g., "if Finance team, route to CFO") |
| **Task** | Action item assigned to someone |
| **Integration** | Calls an external system (identity provider, MDM, automation platform) |
| **Notification** | Automated email or chat message |

**A well-designed catalog item** asks only necessary questions, auto-populates known data (identity, department, manager), routes automatically to the correct approver, and triggers automation on approval without manual handoff.

### Automation Platforms — Workato & n8n

Enterprise automation platforms connecting SaaS applications and triggering actions between them — visual, drag-and-drop workflow builders rather than requiring code. Example pattern: a new hire in an HRIS automatically triggers account creation in an identity provider, device enrollment in an MDM tool, and channel/license assignment, all from one trigger.

- **Workato** — commercial enterprise automation platform
- **n8n** — open-source equivalent, more developer-friendly, self-hostable; same underlying logic, different interface

### Kandji — Apple-First MDM

An Apple-first MDM platform managing Mac fleets — enrollment, configuration profiles, software deployment, patch management, security policies. Comparable to Jamf, generally regarded as having a more modern interface and stronger built-in automation.

**Key Kandji concepts:**
- **Blueprints** — configuration profiles applied to devices
- **Auto Apps** — automatic software deployment and updates
- **Liftoff** — Kandji's zero-touch onboarding experience for new Macs
- **Passport** — local account management tied to identity provider credentials
- **Prism** — device insights and compliance reporting

### SLA Compliance — What the Numbers Mean

| Priority | Target Resolution Time |
|---|---|
| P1 (Critical) | 4 hours |
| P2 (High) | 8 hours |
| P3 (Medium) | 24 hours |
| P4 (Low) | 5 business days |

SLA compliance percentage reflects the share of tickets resolved within their promised timeframe, typically tracked separately for Incidents (something broken) vs. Requests/Tasks (something needed). Most IT teams operate in the 85–92% range; achieving 98%+ while ticket volume grows is a genuine signal that automation — not added headcount — is absorbing the increased demand.

## Key Takeaways

- **Circle's core bet:** own not just the stablecoin (USDC) but the infrastructure layer it moves on (Arc), positioning as the "Economic OS for the internet"
- **The business model is straightforward:** reserve-backed stablecoin issuance, earning Treasury interest on reserves at scale
- **Circle wins on trust and regulation, not being first** — Tether launched USDC's category years earlier; Circle's differentiation is compliance depth
- **IT stakes are genuinely elevated** — supporting infrastructure for institutional financial transactions carries direct financial and legal consequences for downtime
- **The compliance chain (CMDB → vulnerability management → MDM patching → GRC evidence → SOC 2)** is a clean, reusable architectural pattern applicable well beyond Circle specifically
